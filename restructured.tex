\subsection{Conservation of mass}
The conservation of mass, or continuity equation, for an infinitesimal volume element of a continuum states that
\begin{align}
    \pdv{\rho}{t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\bm{u}) &= 0 \label{eq:cont}
\end{align}
Where $\pdv{\rho}{t}$ represents the change of mass in such a volume element while $\nabla \cdot (\rho\bm{u})$ represents the net flux
of mass into or out of the volume. Various expressions of the mass continuity equations which are used by different authors include 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(\rho u_i) &= 0\\
\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i} &= 0
\end{align*}
which can be shown to be equivalent to Eq.~\eqref{eq:cont} using the mathematical formulations presented in Appendix \ref{material_derivative}.

%-----------------------------------
\subsection{Conservation of momentum}
The governing equation for the acceleration of an elemental parcel subjected to external forces is 
\begin{align} \label{eq:momentum_jamc}
    \rho\left(\pdv{\bm{u}}{t} + \bm{u} \cdot \nabla \bm{u}\right) &= -\nabla P + \nabla\cdot {\bm \tau} + \rho \bm{g} 
\end{align}
or, in terms of the material derivative and in tensor notation,
\begin{align} \label{eq:momentum_scto} 
    \rho \mdv{u_i}{t} = - \pdv{P}{x_i} + \pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j}+\rho g_i
\end{align}
The term(s) on the left of Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum_jamc} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum_scto} represent the intertia of the 
volume element, while $\nabla P$ is the pressure gradient force, 
$\pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j}$ the (viscous) deviatoric stresses, and $\rho \bm{g}$ the gravitational (body) force \autocite{scto01}.

\subsection{Conservation of energy}
The relevant governing equation derived from the conservation of energy is written by \textcite{jamc80} as
\begin{align}
    \rho C_p \left\{\pdv{T}{t} - \frac{\alpha T}{\rho C_p}\pdv{P}{t} + \bm{u} \cdot [\nabla T - (\nabla T)_s]\right\} &= 
    \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + {\bm \tau} : \nabla {\bm u} \label{eq:energy_jamc}
\end{align}
Where the terms represent, in order, the local rate of change of thermal energy, the adiabatic heating, 
advection of temperature, diffusion, internal heat generation, and viscous dissipation. 
In their derivation, it is relevant for \textcite{jamc80} to write the energy equation in terms of and define the adiabatic temperature gradient as
\begin{align*}
    (\nabla T)_s = \frac{\alpha T}{\rho C_p} \nabla P
\end{align*}
Using this, Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_jamc} can alternatively be written as
\begin{align*}
    \rho C_p \pdv{T}{t} - \alpha T \pdv{P}{t} +\rho C_p \bm{u} \cdot \nabla T - \alpha T \bm{u}\cdot\nabla P &= \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H +  {\bm \tau} : \nabla {\bm u} \\
    \rho C_p \mdv{T}{t} - \alpha T \mdv{P}{t} &= \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + {\bm \tau} : \nabla {\bm u} 
\end{align*}
which is the energy equation used by \textcite{scto01}. 
Again referring to Appendix \ref{material_derivative}, one can show that Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_jamc} can also be written as
\begin{align*}
    \rho C_p \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + {\bm u} \cdot \nabla T\right) - \alpha T\left(\pdv{P}{t}+{\bm u} \cdot \nabla P\right)  
=  \nabla \cdot(K\nabla T) + H + {\bm \tau}: \dot{\bm \varepsilon} 
\end{align*}
with $\dot{\bm \varepsilon}$ the strain rate tensor. The equation above is of the form used by \textcite{gada20}, the only differences being the omission of 
the latent heat generated by phase transitions, which we do not take into account here, and the addition of $\partial{P}/\partial{t}$ which is neglected by 
\textcite{gada20}\footnote{\textcite{gada20}
does not provide any explicit justification for their omission of $\partial{P}/\partial{t}$}.

\subsection{Viscous rheology}

In a Newtonian fluid, the stress tensor ${\bm \tau}$ is linearly dependent on 
the strain rate tensor $\dot{\bm \varepsilon}$, and for Newtonian as well as isotropic 
fluids \footnote{anisotropic mantle viscosity leha08, leha11, SKS splitting} we can use this relation to 
write the stress tensor elements in terms of the velocity field: 
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress_1}
\tau_{ij} = \eta \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
 - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} \right) 
 + k_B\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}.
\end{align}
where $\eta$ and $k_B$ are the dynamic and bulk viscosity, respectively. 
The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stress_1} is discussed in detail by \textcite{scto01}.
Since the bulk viscosity is associated with rapid changes in volume and volume changes in 
mantle convection occur over timescales greater than $10^7$ years \autocite{jamc80}, the terms with $k_B$ 
are neglected and Eq.~\eqref{eq:stress_1} is reduced to 
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress}
\tau_{ij} = \eta \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
 - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} \right) 
\end{align}
or, 
\[
{\bm \tau} = 2\eta \dot{\bm \varepsilon}^d = 2\eta \left( \dot{\bm \varepsilon}  - \frac13 \text{tr}(\dot{\bm \varepsilon})    {\bm 1} \right)
\]
where $ \dot{\bm \varepsilon}^d$ is the deviatoric strain rate tensor.

\subsection{Adams-Williamson reference state}

Following the approach of \textcite{jamc80} and \textcite{birc52}, an expression for the reference 
density distribution $\rho_r$ is done by considering the gradient of $\rho_r(S, P_H)$ with $S$ 
the entropy per unit mass: 
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla\rho_r(S,P_H) = \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{S}\right)_{P_H} \nabla S + \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P_H}\right)_S\nabla P_H
\end{equation*}
with $\nabla S = 0$ since the reference state is adiabatic. In the radial direction $z$, $\nabla \rho_r = \partial_z \rho_r$ 
and the hydrostatic component of the pressure gradient is given by $\nabla P_H = -\rho_rg \bm{\hat{z}}$ so that 
we can write the equation above as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ref_K}
    \frac{1}{\rho_r} \pdv{\rho_r}{z} = -g \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P_H}\right)_S = -\frac{\rho_r g}{K_S}
\end{equation} 
with $K_S = \rho_r\left(\pdv{P_H}{\rho_r}\right)_S$ the isentropic modulus of bulk compressibility (See definition 
of the isothermal modulus of bulk compressibility $K_T$ in the previous subsection above for reference). 

Using the Grüneisen parameter $\Gamma$ first introduced by \textcite{birc52}
\begin{align}
    \Gamma = \frac{\alpha K_S}{\rho C_p} = \dfrac{\alpha K_T}{\rho C_v},
    \qquad
    \text{with} \qquad
    \frac{K_S}{K_T} = \frac{C_p}{C_v} \label{eq:grun}
\end{align}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ref_K} may be written as the Adams-Williamson relation for a chemically homogeneous adiabatic density distribution 
under hydrostatic pressure \autocite{birc52}:
 \begin{equation} \label{eq:adams-williamson}
    \frac{1}{\rho_r} \pdv{\rho_r}{z} = -\frac{1}{H_T \Gamma}
\end{equation}
with $H_T = C_p/g\alpha$ the temperature scale height of the liquid. This scale height represents the vertical distance over which a parcel of 
liquid must be moved in order to change its temperature (by adiabatic 
expansion or compression) by a factor $e$ \autocite{jamc80}. 


\textcite{jamc80} argues that for most liquids, $\Gamma$ is approximately constant and of order $1$, thus Eq.~\ref{eq:adams-williamson} may
be integrated over $z$ to find
\begin{equation} \label{eq:15}
    \rho_r(z)= \rho_0 e^{(d-z)/H_T \Gamma}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_0 = \rho_r(z=d)$ is the reference density value at the surface and $H_T$ is the temperature scale height of the liquid \autocite{jamc80}.

Plugging Eq.~\eqref{eq:15} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_state} gives the (linearized equation) of state
\begin{equation} \label{eq:full_eq_state}
    \rho(T,P) = \rho_0 e^{(d-z)/H_T \Gamma}[1-\alpha (T-T_s)+K_T^{-1}(P-P_H)]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Intro to scaling}
The governing equations as presented in section \ref{section:general_eq} and \ref{section:eq_state} are in their dimensional from. In order 
to easily perform the simplification of these equations in section \ref{section:ALA}, scaling factors must be introduced to nondimensionalize them. 
Through nondimensionalization we are then able to demonstrate which terms can be neglected when compared in scale to the process we want to resolve in our model 
(in this case, mantle convection).
Physically, this means that we assume that processes that take place on a relatively short timescale, such as seismic waves, do not significantly impact mantle convection.
An additional numerical argument for neglecting terms is that processes on drastically different timescales can usually not be incorporated into a single numerical model, 
since processes that take place on timescales 
shorter than the model timestep cannot be accurately resolved \autocite{gada20}. 

There are multiple approaches for choosing appropriate scaling factors.

\subsection{Dimensionless numbers}
The ultimate goal is to arrive at a set of dimensionless equations that include the Rayleigh number (Ra), the Mach number (M), the Prandtl number (Pr), and the Dissipation number (D). 
Here, we introduce these dimensionless parameters and present their physical interpretation. In the next section \ref{section:ALA}, we will show how these 
parameters emerge naturally through nondimensionalization. 

\textbf{The Rayleigh number (Ra)} is a dimensionless number that characterizes the main thermal transport mode of a system: 
below a critical value, there is no convection and heat transfer in the system is purely conductive. When Ra is above this critical value, heat is also transported by convection. 
Essentially, the Rayleigh number multiplies the buoyancy force term in the momentum equation and controls the vigor of convection \autocite{scto01}. 
Mathematically, the Rayleigh number is defined as the ratio of the timescale of diffusive thermal transport to the timescale of convective velocities \autocite{scto01}.

\textbf{The Dissipation number (Di)} compares the natural scale of temperature variations (temperature scale height) with a layer thickness $d$ \autocite{rica07}. 
\begin{align*}
    \mathit{Di} \equiv \frac{\alpha_0 g d}{C_{p0}} = \frac{d}{H_T}
\end{align*}
Considering the density scale height of the system \autocite{scto01} (see Table \ref{table:variables})
\begin{align*}
    H_\rho = \frac{K_s}{g\rho}
\end{align*}
We can also show that the dissipation number divided by the Grüneisen parameter \eqref{eq:grun} is the ratio of the layer thickness to the density scale height: 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\mathit{Di}}{\Gamma} = \frac{g\rho d}{K_s} = \frac{d}{H_\rho}
\end{align*}
The validity of considering the system to be incompressible, and thus the validity of the Boussinesq approximation, requires $\mathit{Di}/\Gamma \ll 1$ \autocite{scto01}.
Since the Grüneisen parameter is approximately constant and unity, many reduce this requirement to $\mathit{Di} \ll 1$ \autocite{jamc80,rica07}. We will see in section \ref{section:ALA} that
the terms scaling with the dissipation number are dropped when applying the Boussinesq approximation.
However, using the values in Table \ref{table:normalizing_qt}, we arrive at $\mathit{Di} \approx 0.75$, and the Boussinesq approximation is only approximately valid. 


\textbf{The Mach number (M)} compares the characteristic speed of an object or fluid $U$ to the local speed of sound in the surrounding medium $C$. 
\begin{align*}
    M^2 = \frac{U^2}{C^2}
\end{align*}

The Mach number is often used in aviation, where the speed of an object is 
compared to the speed of sound in the atmosphere. Here, however, we have some freedom to chose the characteristic speed. 
Some authors choose $U$ to be a Stokes (convective) velocity, derived from the vorticity equation \autocite{mcrw74}, $U_S \approx 300 \text{m yr}^{-1}$ \autocite{jamc80}, which is very fast compared compared to geological scales. Others choose $U$ 
as the characteristic velocity of thermal 
diffusion (based on the diffusive timescale) $U_D = \kappa_0/d \approx 7 \times 10^{-6} \text{m yr}^{-1}$ \autocite{rica07,scto01,itki94,lezh08}, which is very slow compared to geological scales. 
Whether one chooses one velocity scale over the other, both result in a sufficiently small Mach number. Using values from Table \ref{table:normalizing_qt} and the diffusive velocity as characteristic velocity scale,

\subsection{Continuity equation}
In this section, we show that how elastic (seismic) waves can be eliminated from the governing equations through a scaling argument\footnote{\textcite{jamc80} 
do not provide a derivation for their elimination of $\pdv{\rho}{t}$. This section follows the derivation
by \textcite{scto01} but with the notation changed to match that of \textcite{jamc80}}. 
Our starting point is the linearized equation of state \eqref{eq:eq_state}: 
\begin{align*}
    \rho= \rho_r \left[ 1 - \alpha\,(T-T_S) + K_T^{-1}\,(P-P_H) \right]
\end{align*}

which we can write in dimensionless form by plugging in values from Table \ref{table:variables}:
\begin{align}
    &=\rho'_r\left[1- \alpha T_0(T'-T'_s)+\frac{\eta \kappa_0}{K_Td^2}(P'-P'_H) \right] \notag \\
    &=\rho'_r\left[[1+ \frac{\rho_0 \kappa_0^2}{K_T d^2}\frac{\nu_0}{\kappa_0}P'_1-\alpha T_0 T'_1]\right] \notag\\ 
    &=\rho_r\left[1+P_1M^2Pr - \mu T_1\right] \label{eq:nond_eq_state}\\ 
\end{align}
where we dropped de primes in the last line (all variables are dimensionless), and\footnote{\textcite{jamc80} and \textcite{scto01} define different Mach numbers, 
but since \cite{jamc80} does not 
elaborate their chosen acoustic (seismic) wave velocity, we use the Mach number as defined by \cite{scto01} in this section.}
\begin{align*}
    M^2 = \frac{\kappa_0^2\rho_0}{K_T d^2}, \qquad Pr = \frac{\nu_0}{\kappa_0}, \qquad \mu=\alpha T_0 
\end{align*}  
\textcite{jamc80} shows a different approach, using the Grüneisen parameter \eqref{eq:grun}, an explicit adiabatic temperature profile, and the thermodynamical relation 
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}
to arrive at

Now we turn to the energy equation. Our goal is to write it in dimensionless form and in such a way that we can take the limit of $\mu \to 0$ and reduce it.

\end{align*}

\subsection{Material derivative} \label{material_derivative}
For many authors, it is convenient to introduce the material derivative  
\begin{align*}
\frac{D}{Dt} &= \frac{\partial }{\partial t}+ u \frac{\partial }{\partial x} + v \frac{\partial }{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial }{\partial z}\\
&= \frac{\partial }{\partial t} + \textbf{u}\cdot \nabla  \\
&= \frac{\partial }{\partial t} + u_i \pdv{} {x_i} \\
\end{align*}
where $D/Dt$ is the time derivative \textbf{following the motion of an element of fluid}. $\partial/\partial t$ is the local time derivative, 
whereas $u_i \pdv{} {x_i}$ are the spatial derivatives, which capture the change in the fluid element as it moves along a trajectory 
through a spatially varying field. For example, a fluid elements temperature can change in time, and when it is transported into a colder region.

\subsection{Stress tensor} \label{stress_tensor}
There are many different ways to write the dissipation term in the energy equation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_jamc}). To understand these varying notations, 
we present an overview of different tensor notations and operations below. 

\begin{align*}
\tau	_{ij}\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = \boldsymbol{\tau}:\nabla\textbf{u}
\end{align*}
where 
\begin{align*}
\nabla\mathbf{u} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z} \\[2mm]
\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial z} \\[2mm]
\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial z}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}

and 
\begin{align*}
    \bm{\tau} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
  \tau_{11} & \tau_{12} & \tau_{13} \\
  \tau_{12} & \tau_{22} & \tau_{23} \\
  \tau_{13} & \tau_{23} & \tau_{33}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
where we used that $\bm{\tau}$ is a symmetric tensor and thus $\tau_{ij} = \tau_{ji}$.  
The : is called the double contraction operator or the double dot products. For two second-order tensors (matrices), the double contraction yields a scalar by summing over 
two pairs of indices.
