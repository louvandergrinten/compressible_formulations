\section{General equations} \label{section:general_eq}
\subsection{Conservational laws}
\subsubsection{Conservation of mass}
The conservation of mass for an infinitesimal volume element of a continuum states that
\begin{align}
    \pdv{\rho}{t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\bm{u}) &= 0\\ \label{eq:mass}
\end{align}
Where $\pdv{\rho}{t}$ represents the change of mass in such a volume element while $\nabla \cdot (\rho\bm{u})$ represents the net flux
of mass into or out of the volume. Various expressions of the mass continuity equations which are used by different authors include 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(\rho u_i) &= 0\\
\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i} &= 0
\end{align*}
which can be shown to be equivalent to Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass} using the mathematical formulations presented in Appendix \ref{section:math}.


\subsubsection{Conservation of momentum}
The governing equation for the acceleration of an elemental parcel subjected to external forces is 
\begin{align} \label{eq:momentum_jamc}
    \rho\left(\pdv{\bm{u}}{t} + \bm{u} \cdot \nabla \bm{u}\right) &= -\nabla P + \pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j} + \rho \bm{g} 
\end{align}
or, in terms of the material derivative and in tensor notation,
\begin{align} \label{eq:momentum_scto} 
    \rho \mdv{u_i}{t} = - \pdv{P}{x_i} + \pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j}+\rho g_i
\end{align}
The term(s) on the left of Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum_jamc} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum_scto} represent the intertia of the 
volume element, while $\nabla P$ is the pressure gradient force, 
$\pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j}$ the (viscous) deviatoric stresses, and $\rho \bm{g}$ the gravitational (body) force \autocite{scto01}. 

\subsubsection{Conservation of energy}
The relevant governing equation derived from the conservation of energy is written by \textcite{jamc80} as
\begin{align}
    \rho C_p \left\{\pdv{T}{t} - \frac{\alpha T}{\rho C_p}\pdv{P}{t} + \bm{u} \cdot [\nabla T - (\nabla T)_s]\right\} &= 
    \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + \tau_{ij}\pdv{u_i}{u_{ij}} \label{eq:energy_jamc}
\end{align}
\textcolor{green}{check}Where the terms represent, in order, the local rate of change of thermal energy, the adiabatic heating, 
advection of temperature, diffusion, internal heat generation, and viscous dissipation. 
In their derivation, it is relevant for \textcite{jamc80} to write the energy equation in terms of and define the adiabatic temperature gradient as
\begin{align*}
    (\nabla T)_s = \frac{\alpha T}{\rho C_p} \nabla P
\end{align*}
Using this, Eq.~\ref{eq:energy_jamc} can alternatively be written as
\begin{align*}
    \rho C_p \pdv{T}{t} - \alpha T \pdv{\rho}{t} +\rho C_p \bm{u} \cdot \nabla T - \alpha T \bm{u}\cdot\nabla P &= \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + \tau_{ij}\pdv{u_i}{u_{ij}}\\
    \rho C_p \mdv{T}{t} - \alpha T \mdv{\rho}{t} &= \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + \tau_{ij}\pdv{u_i}{u_{ij}}
\end{align*}
Which is the energy equation used by \textcite{scto01}. 
Referring to Appendix \ref{section:math}, one can show that Eq.~\ref{eq:energy_jamc} can also be written as
\begin{align*}
    \rho C_p \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \textbf{u} \cdot \nabla T\right) - \alpha T(\textbf{u} \cdot \nabla P)  =  \nabla \cdot(K\nabla T) + H +\tau: \dot{\varepsilon} 
\end{align*}
With $\dot{\varepsilon}$ the strain rate, which is adapted from the energy equation used by \textcite{gada20}.  

\subsection{Stress and strain}
In a Newtonian fluids, the stress tensor elements $\tau_{ij}$ are linearly dependent on the strain rate $e_{ij}$, and for Newtonian as well as isotropic fluids \textcolor{green}{explain what this means or is this too in-depth?} we can use this relation to 
write the stress tensor elements in terms of the velocity field: 
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress_1}
\tau_{ij} = \eta \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
 - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} \right) 
 + k_B\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}.
\end{align}
With $\eta$ and $k_B$ the dynamic and bulk viscosity, respectively. The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stress_1} is discussed in detail by \textcite{scto01}.
Since the bulk viscosity is associated with rapid changes in volume and volume changes in 
mantle convection occur over timescales greater than $10^7$ years \autocite{jamc80}, the terms with $k_B$ are neglected and Eq.~\eqref{eq:stress_1} is reduced to 
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress}
\tau_{ij} = \eta \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
 - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} \right) 
\end{align}

\section{Equation of state} \label{section:eq_state}
\subsection{Linearizing the equation of state}
A major simplification of the general equations described in section \ref{section:general_eq} derives from the linearization of the equation of state, which describes the density of the material as a function of state variables. Neglecting 
density changes associated with phase transitions such as the olivine-spinel transition, as investigated by \textcite{itki94}, the density of the mantle material can be defined as a function of only temperature and pressure $\rho = \rho(T, P)$. 
The mantle density increases by approximately 65\% between the surface 
and the core-mantle boundary, and most of this change is due to hydrostatic compression \autocite{scto01}. This is why it is justified for many approximating methods 
to assume a time-independent and adiabatic reference density profile, which remains fixed throughout the calculation \autocite{itki94}. We can therefore expand the 
pressure, temperature and density as
\begin{align*}
    T &= T_s + T_1 \\
    P &= P_H + P_1 \\
    \rho(T,P) &= \rho_r + \rho'(P_1, T_1)
\end{align*}
Where $P_H$ is the hydrostatic pressure and $P_1$ deviations from it, $T_s$ is the adiabatic temperature component and $T_1$ deviations from it, and $\rho_r$ is a
time-independent adiabatic reference profile. The deviations from this profile need not be 
necessarily small \autocite{scto01}, but assuming that they are, the equation of state can be written 
as a linear Taylor expansionof the density $\rho$ about the reference state $\rho_r$: 
\begin{align*} \label{eq:eq_state}
    \rho(T,P) &= \rho_r + \left(\pdv{\rho}{P}\right)_T P' + \left(\pdv{\rho}{T}\right)_P T' \\
    &= \rho_r[1-\alpha(T-T_s)+K_T^{-1}(P-P_H)] 
\end{align*}
Where 
\begin{align*}
    \alpha &= - \frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\pdv{\rho_R}{T}\right)_P \\
    K_T &= \rho_r \left(\pdv{P}{\rho_r}\right)_T 
\end{align*}
Which are the thermal expansion coefficient and the isothermal modulus of bulk compressibility, respectively (i.e. coefficients that determine how the density varies with changes in temperature and pressure)(Table \ref{table:variables}; \cite{jamc80}). 
What remains is to determine an expression for reference density profile $\rho_r$. This can be done in numerous ways, but many authors (\cite{jamc80}; \cite{lezh08}; \cite{itki94}) choose to follow the method first
published by \textcite{birc52}, which is expanded below. However, one should 
keep in mind that other expressions for the reference state are possible, such as the polytropic reference state presented by \textcite{glat88}. 

\subsection{Adams-Williamson reference state}
Following the approach of \textcite{jamc80} and \textcite{birc52}, an expression for the reference density distribution $\rho_r$ is done by considering the gradient of $\rho_r(S, P_H)$ with $S$ 
the entropy per unit mass: 
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla\rho_R(S,P_H) = \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{S}\right)_{P_H} \nabla S + \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P_H}\right)_S\nabla P_H
\end{equation*}
With $\nabla S = 0$ since the reference state is adiabatic, and with the hydrostatic component of the pressure gradient $\nabla P_H = -\rho_rg \bm{\hat{z}}$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ref_K}
    \frac{1}{\rho_r} \pdv{\rho_r}{z} = -g \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P_H}\right)_S = -\frac{\rho_r g}{K_s}
\end{equation} 
with $K_s = \rho_r\left(\pdv{P_H}{\rho_r}\right)_s$ the isentropic modulus of bulk compressibility. 

Using the Grüneisen parameter $\Gamma$ first introduced by \textcite{birc52}
\begin{align}
    \Gamma = \frac{\alpha K_s}{\rho C_p} = \dfrac{\alpha K_T}{\rho C_v}
\end{align}
 Eq.~\eqref{eq:ref_K} may be written as the Adams-Williamson relation for a chemically homogeneous adiabatic density distribution under hydrostatic pressure \autocite{birc52}:
 \begin{equation} \label{eq:adams-williamson}
    \frac{1}{\rho_r} \pdv{\rho_r}{z} = -\frac{1}{H_T \Gamma}
\end{equation}
\textcite{jamc80} argues that for most liquids, $\Gamma$ is approximately constant and of order $1$, thus Eq.~\ref{eq:adams-williamson} may
be integrated over $z$ to find
\begin{equation} \label{eq:15}
    \rho_r(z)= \rho_0 e^{(d-z)/H_T \Gamma}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_0 = \rho_r(z=d)$ is the reference density value at the surface and $H_T$ is the temperature scale height of the liquid \autocite{jamc80}.

The governing equations as presented in section \ref{section:general_eq} and \ref{section:eq_state} are in their dimensional from. In order 
to easily perform the simplification of these equations in section \ref{section:ALA}, scaling factors must be introduced to nondimensionalize them. 
Through nondimensionalization we are then able to demonstrate which terms can be neglected when compared in scale to the process we want to resolve in our model (in this case, mantle convection).
Physically, this means that we assume that processes that take place on a relatively short timescale, such as seismic waves, do not significantly impact mantle convection.
An additional numerical argument is that processes on drastically different timescales can usually not be incorporated into a single numerical model, since processes that take place on timescales 
shorter than the model timestep cannot be accurately resolved \autocite{gada20}.
