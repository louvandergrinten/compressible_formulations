\subsection{Conservation of mass}
The conservation of mass for an infinitesimal volume element of a continuum states that
\begin{align}
    \pdv{\rho}{t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\bm{u}) &= 0\\ \label{eq:mass}
\end{align}
Where $\pdv{\rho}{t}$ represents the change of mass in such a volume element while $\nabla \cdot (\rho\bm{u})$ represents the net flux
of mass into or out of the volume. Various expressions of the mass continuity equations which are used by different authors include 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(\rho u_i) &= 0\\
\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i} &= 0
\end{align*}
which can be shown to be equivalent to Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass} using the mathematical formulations presented in Appendix \ref{section:math}.


\subsection{Conservation of momentum}
The governing equation for the acceleration of an elemental parcel subjected to external forces is 
\begin{align} \label{eq:momentum_jamc}
    \rho\left(\pdv{\bm{u}}{t} + \bm{u} \cdot \nabla \bm{u}\right) &= -\nabla P + \nabla\cdot {\bm \tau} + \rho \bm{g} 
\end{align}
or, in terms of the material derivative and in tensor notation,
\begin{align} \label{eq:momentum_scto} 
    \rho \mdv{u_i}{t} = - \pdv{P}{x_i} + \pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j}+\rho g_i
\end{align}
The term(s) on the left of Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum_jamc} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum_scto} represent the intertia of the 
volume element, while $\nabla P$ is the pressure gradient force, 
$\pdv{\tau_{ij}}{x_j}$ the (viscous) deviatoric stresses, and $\rho \bm{g}$ the gravitational (body) force \autocite{scto01}. 

\subsection{Conservation of energy}
The relevant governing equation derived from the conservation of energy is written by \textcite{jamc80} as
\begin{align}
    \rho C_p \left\{\pdv{T}{t} - \frac{\alpha T}{\rho C_p}\pdv{P}{t} + \bm{u} \cdot [\nabla T - (\nabla T)_s]\right\} &= 
    \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + {\bm \tau} : \nabla {\bm u} \label{eq:energy_jamc}
\end{align}
\textcolor{green}{check}Where the terms represent, in order, the local rate of change of thermal energy, the adiabatic heating, 
advection of temperature, diffusion, internal heat generation, and viscous dissipation. 
In their derivation, it is relevant for \textcite{jamc80} to write the energy equation in terms of and define the adiabatic temperature gradient as
\begin{align*}
    (\nabla T)_s = \frac{\alpha T}{\rho C_p} \nabla P
\end{align*}
Using this, Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_jamc} can alternatively be written as
\begin{align*}
    \rho C_p \pdv{T}{t} - \alpha T \pdv{P}{t} +\rho C_p \bm{u} \cdot \nabla T - \alpha T \bm{u}\cdot\nabla P &= \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H +  {\bm \tau} : \nabla {\bm u} \\
    \rho C_p \mdv{T}{t} - \alpha T \mdv{P}{t} &= \nabla \cdot (K\nabla T) + H + {\bm \tau} : \nabla {\bm u} 
\end{align*}
which is the energy equation used by \textcite{scto01}. 
Referring to Appendix \ref{section:math}, one can show that Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_jamc} can also be written as
\begin{align*}
    \rho C_p \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + {\bm u} \cdot \nabla T\right) - \alpha T({\bm u} \cdot \nabla P)  
=  \nabla \cdot(K\nabla T) + H + {\bm \tau}: \dot{\bm \varepsilon} 
\end{align*}

\subsection{Viscous rheology}

In a Newtonian fluid, the stress tensor ${\bm \tau}$ is linearly dependent on 
the strain rate tensor $\dot{\bm \varepsilon}$, and for Newtonian as well as isotropic 
fluids \footnote{anisotropic mantle viscosity leha08, leha11, SKS splitting} we can use this relation to 
write the stress tensor elements in terms of the velocity field: 
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress_1}
\tau_{ij} = \eta \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
 - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} \right) 
 + k_B\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}.
\end{align}
where $\eta$ and $k_B$ are the dynamic and bulk viscosity, respectively. 
The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stress_1} is discussed in detail by \textcite{scto01}.
Since the bulk viscosity is associated with rapid changes in volume and volume changes in 
mantle convection occur over timescales greater than $10^7$ years \autocite{jamc80}, the terms with $k_B$ 
are neglected and Eq.~\eqref{eq:stress_1} is reduced to 
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress}
\tau_{ij} = \eta \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
 - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} \right) 
\end{align}
or, 
\[
{\bm \tau} = 2\eta \dot{\bm \varepsilon}^d = 2\eta \left( \dot{\bm \varepsilon}  - \frac13 \text{tr}(\dot{\bm \varepsilon})    {\bm 1} \right)
\]
where $ \dot{\bm \varepsilon}^d$ is the deviatoric strain rate tensor.

\subsection{Linearizing the equation of state}

A major simplification of the general equations described in section \ref{section:general_eq} derives from the 
linearization of the equation of state, which describes the density of the material as a function of state variables. 
Neglecting density changes associated with phase transitions such as the olivine-spinel transition {\color{red} figure + caption explaining }, 
as investigated by \textcite{itki94}, the density of the mantle material can be defined as a function of only 
temperature and pressure $\rho = \rho(T, P)$. The mantle density increases by approximately 65\% between the surface 
and the core-mantle boundary {\color{red} as shown in Fig dzan81} , and most of this change is due to hydrostatic 
compression \autocite{scto01}. This is why it is justified for many approximating methods 
to assume a time-independent and adiabatic reference density profile, which remains fixed throughout the 
calculation \autocite{itki94}. We can therefore expand the pressure, temperature and density as
\begin{align*}
    T &= T_s + T_1 \\
    P &= P_H + P_1 \\
    \rho(T,P) &= \rho_r + \rho'(P_1, T_1)
\end{align*}
where $P_H$ is the hydrostatic pressure and $P_1$ deviations from it, 
$T_s$ is the adiabatic temperature component and $T_1$ deviations from it, and $\rho_r$ is a
time-independent adiabatic reference profile. 
The deviations from this profile need not be 
necessarily small \autocite{scto01}, but assuming that they are, the equation of state can be written 
as a linear Taylor expansion of the density $\rho$ about the reference state $\rho_r$
(thereby enabling us to arrive at the exact form for the 3rd equation above):

The Taylor expansion of a 2-variable function in the vicinity of a point $x_0,y_0$ is given by
\[
f(x,y) = f(x_0,y_0) 
+ \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x_0,y_0} (x-x_0)
+ \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right|_{x_0,y_0} (y-y_0) + \dots
\]
Replacing now $f \rightarrow \rho$, $x \rightarrow T$,  $y \rightarrow P$,  
$x_0 \rightarrow T_S$ and 
$y_0 \rightarrow P_H$ leads to 
\begin{align*} \label{eq:eq_state}
\rho(T,P) 
&= \underbrace{\rho(T_S,P_H)}_{\rho_r}  + \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial T} (T_S,P_H)  \right)_P  (T-T_s)  
+ \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial P} (T_S,P_H)\right)_T (P-P_H) \\
%&= \rho_r + \left(\pdv{\rho}{T}\right)_P T_1  + \left(\pdv{\rho}{P}\right)_T P_1 \\
&= \rho_r \left[ 1 + 
\frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial T} (T_S,P_H) \right)_P (T-T_s)  + 
\frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial P} (T_S,P_H) \right)_T (P-P_H) \right] \\
&= \rho_r[1-\alpha(T-T_s)+K_T^{-1}(P-P_H)] 
\end{align*}
where\footnote{Adding the P and T subscripts to the partial derivatives 
is not mathematically strictly needed but it is common in thermodynamics so
we leave these to match publications} 
\begin{align*}
    \alpha 
&= - \frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial T} (T_S,P_H) \right)_P 
= - \frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{T}\right)_P 
\\
K_T^{-1} 
&= \frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial P} (T_S,P_H) \right)_T 
= \frac{1}{\rho_r} \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P}\right)_T 
\end{align*}
which are the thermal expansion coefficient\footnote{In jamc80 they forget minus sign} 
and the isothermal modulus of bulk compressibility, respectively (i.e. coefficients that determine how 
the density varies with changes in temperature and pressure)(Table \ref{table:variables}; \cite{jamc80}). 
What remains is to determine an expression for reference density profile $\rho_r$. 
This can be done in numerous ways, but many authors (\cite{jamc80}; \cite{lezh08}; \cite{itki94}) choose to follow the method first
published by \textcite{birc52}, which is expanded below. However, one should 
keep in mind that other expressions for the reference state are possible, such as the polytropic reference state presented by \textcite{glat88}.

\subsection{Adams-Williamson reference state}

Following the approach of \textcite{jamc80} and \textcite{birc52}, an expression for the reference 
density distribution $\rho_r$ is done by considering the gradient of $\rho_r(S, P_H)$ with $S$ 
the entropy per unit mass: 
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla\rho_r(S,P_H) = \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{S}\right)_{P_H} \nabla S + \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P_H}\right)_S\nabla P_H
\end{equation*}
with $\nabla S = 0$ since the reference state is adiabatic. In the radial direction $z$, $\nabla \rho_r = \partial_z \rho_r$ 
and the hydrostatic component of the pressure gradient is given by $\nabla P_H = -\rho_rg \bm{\hat{z}}$ so that 
we can write the equation above as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ref_K}
    \frac{1}{\rho_r} \pdv{\rho_r}{z} = -g \left(\pdv{\rho_r}{P_H}\right)_S = -\frac{\rho_r g}{K_S}
\end{equation} 
with $K_S = \rho_r\left(\pdv{P_H}{\rho_r}\right)_S$ the isentropic modulus of bulk compressibility (See definition 
of the isothermal modulus of bulk compressibility $K_T$ in the previous subsection above for reference). 

Using the Grüneisen parameter $\Gamma$ first introduced by \textcite{birc52}
\begin{align}
    \Gamma = \frac{\alpha K_S}{\rho C_p} = \dfrac{\alpha K_T}{\rho C_v}
\qquad

\end{align}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ref_K} may be written as the Adams-Williamson relation for a chemically homogeneous adiabatic density distribution under hydrostatic pressure \autocite{birc52}:
 \begin{equation} \label{eq:adams-williamson}
    \frac{1}{\rho_r} \pdv{\rho_r}{z} = -\frac{1}{H_T \Gamma}
\end{equation}

The governing equations as presented in section \ref{section:general_eq} and \ref{section:eq_state} are in their dimensional from. In order 
to easily perform the simplification of these equations in section \ref{section:ALA}, scaling factors must be introduced to nondimensionalize them. 
Through nondimensionalization we are then able to demonstrate which terms can be neglected when compared in scale to the process we want to resolve in our model (in this case, mantle convection).
Physically, this means that we assume that processes that take place on a relatively short timescale, such as seismic waves, do not significantly impact mantle convection.
An additional numerical argument is that processes on drastically different timescales can usually not be incorporated into a single numerical model, since processes that take place on timescales 
shorter than the model timestep cannot be accurately resolved \autocite{gada20}.
